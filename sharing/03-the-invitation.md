# The invitation

Two offers. Both deliberately small enough to accept without a decision, a budget, or
anyone's permission.

---

## 1 · The one thing to try

> **In your standup, walk the board right to left, oldest item first. For the oldest
> item, ask: what is stopping this finishing today?**
>
> Don't go round the people.

That's it. The entire ask.

**Why this one:** it is free, reversible, needs no tooling, no permission and no data, and
it can be abandoned next week with nothing lost. It is also, per effort spent, the
highest-value single change in the whole model — it reorients a team from *individual
activity* to *collective completion*, which is the shift everything else depends on.

**What to warn them about:** it feels odd for about a week, and it tends to decay back to
a status round-robin within six weeks unless someone keeps reinforcing it. Say that up
front — it makes the decay recognisable rather than a quiet failure.

**What they'll probably notice first:** how much has been sitting there a lot longer than
anyone thought, and how often the honest answer to "what's stopping this" is something
nobody had named.

---

## 2 · The standing offer

> **Come and sit in on one of our flow reviews. No commitment — just come and watch.**

Thirty minutes. Nothing to prepare. They can come once and never again.

Observation beats explanation with a curious audience, because people convince
themselves far more readily than they accept being convinced. Seeing a team talk about an
item that's been open eleven days — without anyone getting defensive — communicates more
than any slide about psychological safety would.

**Before they come**, say what they'll see, so the format doesn't read as chaos:
oldest-first, item-focused, thirty minutes, and the facilitator asks about items rather
than people.

**Don't debrief them afterwards.** Let them draw their own conclusions and ask their own
questions. A post-match explanation turns an observation back into a pitch.

---

## What not to offer

**Don't offer to run it for them.** A cadence someone else runs belongs to that person,
and it stops the day they stop. The point is that the team ends up owning it.

**Don't offer the document set.** Sixteen files reads as a programme. If they want more
after the talk, send [`../OVERVIEW.md`](../OVERVIEW.md) — one page, and it stands alone.

**Don't offer to set it up for another team** until there are results from the first one.
See the last entry in `02-questions-and-pushback.md`.

---

## Keeping the door open

Curious teams move on their own timescale, and mostly when something breaks for them.
The realistic job here is to be **the obvious person to call when it does**, not to
convert anyone now.

So: keep the offer open without repeating it. One mention, then let it sit. Following up
turns a standing invitation into a campaign, and people stop answering.
