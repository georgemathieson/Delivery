# Questions and pushback

Answer these honestly. A defensive answer to any one of them loses the room, and most of
these objections are at least partly right.

---

### "Isn't this just Kanban?"

Largely, yes. The measures are Daniel Vacanti's, the WIP thinking is Little's Law, the
forecasting is standard Monte Carlo. Nothing here is new.

Say so early and without hedging. Claiming novelty invites someone to prove you wrong,
and you'd lose. The claim isn't invention — it's that we hadn't actually been *doing* it,
despite believing we were.

---

### "We already right-size / already do Monte Carlo."

So did we. That's precisely why it's worth talking about — we had all the practices and
they hadn't changed delivery, and we couldn't work out why for a long time.

Good follow-up question to hand them: *does your throughput history include unplanned
work?* If not, the Monte Carlo is forecasting a team with more capacity than the one that
exists — a technically correct method on a systematically optimistic input.

---

### "Our work is different / more complex / more unpredictable."

Might well be. The method doesn't assume otherwise — it measures what your distribution
actually looks like rather than telling you what it should be. If your 95th percentile is
genuinely five times your 50th, that's the finding, and it's worth knowing precisely
rather than approximately.

Resist arguing this one. It's usually a proxy for "don't come and tell us we're slow",
and the answer to that is the next question.

---

### "This sounds like surveillance."

The most important objection in the list, and the most legitimate. Answer it directly
rather than reassuring.

> "It would be, if it were used that way. The measures are about items, never people. In
> our reviews we ask what's stopping an item, not how someone's getting on — and we don't
> compare teams, because different work makes the numbers meaningless across contexts.
>
> I'll be straight with you: the first time one of these charts gets used to question a
> person's output, people start managing the chart instead of the work, the data stops
> being honest, and it never recovers. That's not a risk I'm managing. It's the thing
> that would kill it."

If the room stays sceptical, that's reasonable — it depends on trust they have no reason
to extend yet. Don't over-argue it. Invite them to watch a review and judge for
themselves.

---

### "Will it make us go faster?"

Unknown, and it isn't the aim. It should make you **more predictable**, which is a
different thing — a narrower spread between your fastest and slowest items.

Limiting WIP usually does reduce cycle time, so things often do land sooner. But if you
need a promise about speed, this isn't it.

---

### "Who's making us do this?"

Nobody. It's one team, and it's an experiment. If it doesn't work I'll say so.

Keep this true. The moment it becomes a mandate, it's finished — teams will produce the
charts and ignore them.

---

### "How long before we'd see anything?"

About six weeks to a **diagnosis** — knowing what's actually causing the problem. That
part is close to reliable.

Changing it is slower and less certain: three months to move one lever measurably, six to
see the spread narrow. And there's a real chance it points at something the team doesn't
control, in which case you've learned that instead.

---

### "We can't limit WIP, we've got too many priorities."

That isn't an objection, it's the finding — and it's the most common one.

If a team can't limit its own WIP because of how much work is arriving, the constraint is
upstream of the team, and no board discipline fixes it. Worth knowing, because it changes
who needs to be in the conversation.

---

### "What if we try it and it doesn't work?"

Then you've got four weeks of data about your own delivery that you didn't have, and you
stop. Nothing here is one-way.

The only genuinely wasted version is doing it for three months without ever acting on
what it shows.

---

### "Can you come and set this up for us?"

Tempting, and mostly the wrong answer while it's still unproven on one team.

> "Let me get results first. If it works I'd rather show you what actually happened than
> what I hoped would happen — and if it doesn't, I'd rather not have cost you the time."

If they're genuinely keen, give them the standup change and the invitation to observe.
That's enough to start, costs nothing, and they own it rather than receiving it.
