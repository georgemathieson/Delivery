# Sharing this with other teams

Material for talking to teams who are **curious but not committed**, and who are not
going to change next week.

## The one decision that shapes everything else

**Do not present the playbook.** Present the problem and the experiment.

At the time of writing there are no results. Presenting a method you cannot yet evidence,
to people who have not felt the problem, spends credibility you will want later and buys
very little — an unevidenced method gets polite agreement and no change.

So: two conversations, months apart.

| | When | What | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Talk 1** | Now | The problem, and what we're trying about it | Try one small thing |
| **Talk 2** | 6 months | What actually happened, including what didn't work | Nothing — let them come to you |

Talk 1 costs nothing if the experiment fails and earns a great deal if it works. Talk 2
is the one that changes minds, and it only exists if you keep the calibration record and
the experiment log.

## Principles

**Lead with shared pain, not method.** "We did all the right things and delivery didn't
improve" is a sentence every delivery person in the organisation has lived and nobody has
said out loud. That is the hook. A framework is not.

**Frame it as an experiment, and mean it.** Say what you expect, say how you'd know you
were wrong, and say you'll come back either way. This is both honest and the strongest
position available — it cannot be falsified into embarrassment, and it models the
behaviour you want from them.

**Make the ask small enough to say yes to without a decision.** Not "adopt this". One
cheap, reversible thing they can try on Monday (`03-the-invitation.md`).

**Offer observation over explanation.** "Come and sit in on a flow review" beats any
slide. Curious people convince themselves; they rarely accept being convinced.

**Don't claim novelty.** Most of this is Kanban and Vacanti's flow metrics. Saying so
early disarms the "isn't this just…?" objection and costs nothing — the value is in
applying it, not inventing it.

**Never compare teams.** Not in a talk, not in a chart, not in passing. The moment
another team sees a number of theirs next to a number of yours, this becomes a threat and
the conversation is over.

## The thing to actively avoid

**Do not let this become a mandate.** If leadership sees the talk and decides every team
will now do flow metrics, it dies — teams will produce the charts and ignore them, and
the measures stop being honest the first time they are used to compare or assess.

If someone senior offers to roll it out, the right answer is: *"It's an experiment on one
team so far. Let me come back with results and then we can decide whether it's worth
anyone else's time."* Protect its status as voluntary for as long as you can. Being known
as the person with a delivery framework is worth much less than being the person whose
teams got predictable.

## When you have results

Come back and add `04-what-happened.md`: what you predicted, what occurred, what you got
wrong. Include the failed experiments by name. A talk that includes what didn't work is
believed; a talk that doesn't, isn't.
